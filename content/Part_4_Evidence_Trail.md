## Evidence 02 — Family Background Created Hidden Advantages

**Source type:** Scholarly research article

**Historical evidence:**

Qin Jiang and James Kai-sing Kung (2021) studied late imperial examination outcomes between 1796 and 1905. They found that fathers' education predicted candidates' chances of passing the metropolitan examination, while fathers' and ancestors' official status helped predict final palace examination rankings.

Their research also found that direct wealth measures were not similarly predictive of examination success.

**My interpretation:**

Family advantages were not limited to money. Candidates from educated or official families could inherit knowledge, educational traditions, and preparation that helped them succeed in the examinations. These advantages could be passed from one generation to another.

**Connection to my research question:**

This evidence suggests that examinations could reward academic ability while still reproducing social advantages associated with family background. Even when candidates followed the same examination rules, they did not necessarily begin with the same educational opportunities.

**Source:**

Jiang, Q., & Kung, J. K.-S. (2021). Social mobility in late imperial China: Reconsidering the "ladder of success" hypothesis. *Modern China, 47*(5), 628–661.

[Read the publisher's article](https://doi.org/10.1177/0097700420914529)

**Source limitation:** The study focuses on late imperial examination outcomes from 1796 to 1905. Its findings should not automatically be applied to every period of the Ming and Qing dynasties. The absence of a similar predictive relationship for the direct wealth measures tested does not mean that economic resources were irrelevant to educational access.

## Evidence 03 — Examination Results and Official Networks

**Source type:** Scholarly research article

**Historical evidence:**

Ying Bai and Ruixue Jia examine the relationship between examination performance and official career advancement during the Qing dynasty. Their research indicates that stronger examination performance increased the likelihood of reaching higher government positions. However, advancement also depended on candidates' connections with influential examiners.

The researchers explain that examination performance and these relationships worked together in influencing career success.

**My interpretation:**

Passing an examination was only one stage of advancement. A candidate's later official career could also be influenced by relationships developed within the examination and government system.

However, these relationships should not automatically be considered corruption. Professional connections and personal networks could influence advancement without necessarily involving improper conduct.

**Connection to my research question:**

This evidence shows that merit and social networks could operate together. Even when examinations rewarded academic performance, they did not eliminate every advantage associated with personal relationships and access to influential people.

**Source:**

Bai, Y., & Jia, R. (2025). Meritocracy and the networks it forged: China's imperial examination. *The ANNALS of the American Academy of Political and Social Science, 718*(1), 138–164.

[Read the SAGE publisher article](https://doi.org/10.1177/00027162261473089)

**Publication note:** The journal issue is listed as March 2025, while SAGE records the article's first online publication as September 15, 2026. The citation uses the journal issue year while retaining the online publication date as a qualification.

**Source limitation:** The study examines Qing examination candidates and their subsequent advancement to higher office. It does not represent everyone who attempted to enter the examination system. Its findings should not be treated as proof that all examiner relationships involved favoritism or corruption.

## Evidence 04 — Education and the Privileged Gentry

**Source type:** Scholarly book

**Historical evidence:**

Benjamin A. Elman (2013) explains that classical education and examination preparation were closely connected to educated gentry and merchant families.

In his discussion of the late eighteenth century, he states that only 1% of the growing number of candidates could gain even the provincial degree (p. 125).

He also explains that family lineages and wealthy households could provide better access to classical education, while poorer peasant and artisan families generally could not afford years of preparation (pp. 126–133).

**My interpretation:**

The examination system was highly competitive, and candidates did not begin with equal educational opportunities. Families with established educational traditions and sufficient resources were better positioned to prepare their sons for the examinations.

This suggests that academic achievement was important, but the ability to prepare for the examinations was also influenced by family circumstances.

**Connection to my research question:**

This evidence suggests that examinations could reward academic achievement while continuing to reproduce educational advantages held by established families. The system provided opportunities for advancement, but the preparation required to compete was not equally accessible.

**Source:**

Elman, B. A. (2013). *Civil examinations and meritocracy in late imperial China*. Harvard University Press, pp. 125–133.

ISBN: 9780674724952.

[View the book record on JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt6wppq7)

**Source limitation:** The 1% figure refers to candidates competing for the provincial degree during the late eighteenth century. It does not measure the percentage of the population that became officials or the overall rate of social mobility. The precise candidate denominator and calculation method are not specified in the cited passage. The full-text wording and page location were checked in an accessible digital text copy, but the original printed pages have not been visually inspected.

## Evidence 05 — A Kangxi Edict Criticized Examination Favoritism

**Source type:** Primary historical source

**Historical evidence:**

A 1711 imperial edict issued during the Kangxi reign criticized examination practices in which some assistant examiners did not fully read candidates' examination papers. Instead, they selected papers belonging to favored candidates or candidates with personal connections and submitted them to the chief and deputy examiners.

The edict also connected these problems to the increasing number of examination candidates and insufficient time for examiners to review all the papers carefully. It called for extending the announcement deadline so that examination papers could receive more careful assessment.

**Original Chinese quotation (from the digital transcription):**

> 分校官不將卷子全閲，但檢意中人及有關節卷子，呈送正副考官，主考亦將分校官所呈卷子分别取中，是以真才屈阨。

**English translation:**

Assistant examiners did not read all the examination papers. Instead, they selected papers belonging to candidates they favored and those with personal connections, and submitted them to the chief and deputy examiners. The chief examiners then chose successful candidates from those papers, causing genuinely talented candidates to be overlooked.

## Evidence Balance and Historical Limitations

The five pieces of evidence demonstrate that imperial examinations could create opportunities for social advancement while also maintaining inequalities connected to education, family background, and professional relationships.

Together, they provide evidence of:

- Opportunities for advancement through academic achievement.
- Unequal access to classical education and examination preparation.
- The influence of fathers' education and ancestral official status.
- The importance of examination performance and examiner networks in later official careers.
- Historical concerns about favoritism and examination procedures.

The evidence also has limitations. Some studies focus specifically on late Qing candidates, and the primary imperial edict cannot establish how frequently unfair practices occurred. The available research therefore supports a qualified interpretation rather than a universal conclusion about every period of the Ming and Qing dynasties.
