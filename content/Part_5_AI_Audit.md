# Part 5 — AI Audit

## A. Why I Audited AI

When I first asked ChatGPT about imperial China's examination system, its answers seemed reasonable. It explained that examinations created opportunities for social mobility, but that family background and personal connections could also affect success. However, I wanted to know whether these claims were supported by historical research. I compared the AI-generated claims with academic studies and a Qing primary historical document to see what ChatGPT explained correctly, what it oversimplified, and what it left out.

My initial research did not identify that Elman's 2013 journal article, Evidence 01, had been retracted. The publisher's current record states that the article has been retracted and refers readers to a retraction notice. I keep this source in the audit history because discovering that it was unreliable is part of my investigation, but I do not use it as valid historical evidence. This showed me why checking publication status is essential before relying on academic research. The remaining evidence shows that educational opportunities, fathers' education, inherited official status, examination performance, and later professional networks were not the same thing as simple family wealth.

## B. Claim-by-Claim Audit

### Claim 1: Examinations Created Social Mobility

Original AI claim: The examination system allowed people from families without an established history of government service to enter official careers.

Evaluation: Partially Supported

Historical evidence: Evidence 01 is not used as historical evidence in this audit because the publisher has retracted Elman, B. A. (2013), “The Civil Examination System in Late Imperial China, 1400–1900,” *Frontiers of History in China, 8*(1), 32–50, https://doi.org/10.3868/s020-002-013-0003-9. Jiang and Kung (2021, Evidence 02) find that the late imperial examination system could facilitate mobility, but that ability and family type predicted success in the highest-level examinations. Their study focuses on late imperial examination outcomes from 1796 to 1905.

What AI got right: The examinations provided a route into official life that was not based entirely on inheriting an official position.

What AI missed: A formal opportunity to take examinations did not give all candidates the same chance to succeed. Jiang and Kung found that fathers' education and official family background were associated with different stages of examination success. Their detailed evidence concerns late Qing candidates and should not automatically be applied to every period of the Ming and Qing dynasties.

My corrected understanding: The late imperial examination system created some opportunities for upward mobility, but Jiang and Kung's evidence shows that candidates did not enter the process with the same family and educational advantages.

### Claim 2: Wealthier Families Had Educational Advantages

Original AI claim: Wealthier families could afford tutors, books, education, and more preparation time.

Evaluation: Partially Supported

Historical evidence: Jiang and Kung (2021, Evidence 02) found that higher levels of fathers' education predicted candidates' odds of passing the metropolitan examination. In the palace examination, the official rank of fathers and ancestors predicted final rankings and honors. Their direct proxies for wealth were not similarly predictive. Elman's book (Evidence 04) also explains the importance of classical education and preparation within educated gentry and merchant families.

What AI got right: Educational preparation and family circumstances mattered, and some candidates were better positioned to receive the preparation required for the examinations.

What AI missed: AI treated wealth as if it were the same as every kind of family advantage. Jiang and Kung's findings distinguish direct wealth measures from fathers' education, inherited official background, and family-specific knowledge. The research does not show that money alone determined examination success.

My corrected understanding: Family advantages mattered, but they included educational experience, official background, and cultural knowledge as well as possible economic resources. Wealth alone is not enough to explain examination outcomes.

### Claim 3: Passing Examinations Did Not Guarantee Equal Career Opportunities

Original AI claim: Personal connections and limited government positions affected career advancement after examinations.

Evaluation: Partially Supported

Historical evidence: Bai and Jia (2025, Evidence 03) found that stronger examination performance increased the likelihood of attaining high office among Qing examination candidates, while ties to powerful examiners also mattered for later advancement. The journal issue is dated 2025; the publisher records first online publication on September 15, 2026. A 1711 Kangxi edict (Evidence 05) criticized procedures in which some examination papers were not fully reviewed and favored candidates or candidates with personal connections received attention.

What AI got right: Passing an examination and advancing to higher official positions were not the same thing. Both examination performance and later professional networks could matter for career outcomes.

What AI missed: The evidence does not show that all professional connections were corrupt. Bai and Jia study ties to powerful examiners and do not treat every connection as improper favoritism. The Kangxi edict shows that the Qing government was concerned about unfair examination procedures, but one edict cannot show how frequent such practices were. The approved evidence also does not establish that limited government vacancies caused unequal advancement.

My corrected understanding: Examination performance mattered for later advancement, but professional networks could matter as well. Networks were not automatically corruption, and evidence about limited government positions would require separate research.

## C. Overall Assessment of AI

ChatGPT was useful because it introduced the main historical issues and helped me organize my research. Its three claims were reasonable starting points, but they were too broad to serve as historical conclusions without further investigation. The remaining academic sources showed that family advantages were more complicated than wealth alone and that examination success was different from later official promotion. The Kangxi edict showed that concerns about examination procedures existed at the time, but it cannot prove how common unfair practices were across the entire system.

I also learned that an answer can sound convincing while still needing careful checking. The retraction of Evidence 01 showed why I needed to compare AI claims with what the remaining sources actually said, check the publication status of scholarly work, and distinguish evidence from my own interpretation. AI helped me begin the investigation, but historical conclusions became more reliable only after I verified the sources and their limitations.

## D. My AI Audit Reflection

Before checking the sources, I thought the examination system was mostly fair because candidates had to follow similar rules. After reviewing the evidence, I realized that having the same rules does not necessarily mean having the same opportunities. I also learned that I should not judge an AI answer only by whether it sounds convincing. I need to check what the sources actually say, including original historical documents and the publication status of scholarly articles. Finding that Evidence 01 had been retracted showed me that a source can look useful at first but still be unsuitable as evidence.

For me, the most important lesson is that I need to recognize uncertainty, identify the limits of each source, and separate historical findings from my own interpretation. I also need to check whether a scholarly publication has been retracted or corrected before using it as evidence. AI can help me begin a historical investigation, but it should not replace my own judgment.
